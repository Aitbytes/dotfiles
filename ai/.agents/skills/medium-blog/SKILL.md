## What I do

Write an engaging, reader-focused blog article in the style of Medium's best published posts. The output is a markdown-formatted article that hooks the reader immediately, maintains narrative momentum, and leaves them with something actionable or thought-provoking. Emphasizes clarity, readability, and a human voice while maintaining factual integrity.

## When to use me

When the user wants a publication-ready article for a blog, newsletter, or personal site. Ideal for making research accessible to a general or semi-technical audience. Use when engagement and readability matter more than formal structure or exhaustive citation.

## Tone and Style

- **Conversational but informed**: Write like a smart friend explaining something fascinating, not a professor lecturing.
- **Hook first**: The title and first paragraph must compel the reader to keep going. No preamble.
- **Short paragraphs**: One to three sentences per paragraph. Long blocks of text lose readers.
- **Occasional second person**: "You might be wondering..." is acceptable in moderation.
- **Concrete examples**: Analogies, scenarios, or mini-case studies make abstract findings tangible.
- **Confidence without hedging everything**: Say "researchers found" rather than "it might be suggested that"; but acknowledge genuine uncertainty where it exists.
- **No clickbait**: Titles should be intriguing but honest. Do not over-promise.

## Article Structure

```
# Punchy, Specific Title

**Subtitle that expands but doesn't over-promise**

The opening hook is one or two sentences that establishes tension, a surprising
fact, or a question the reader immediately wants answered. Drop the reader
straight into the topic — no "In this article, we'll explore..."

## The Landscape

Set context: what is the current situation? Why does this matter now?
Paint the picture in 2-3 paragraphs, then state the core problem or opportunity
this research addresses.

## What the Research Shows

The meat of the article. Break findings into digestible sections with
subheadings that advance the narrative. Each section: one key idea, supported
by evidence, with a sentence or two on why it matters.

### Key Finding 1

### Key Finding 2

### What This Means in Practice

## Common Misconceptions or Pitfalls

Address what people often get wrong, or the traps that catch most practitioners.
This shows depth and builds trust.

## So What — The Takeaway

Synthesis. What should the reader do with this information? What decision
does this enable? Keep it concrete: one to three actionable points.

## Open Questions

What remains unknown? What should researchers investigate next? This
demonstrates intellectual honesty and invites reader engagement.

---

*If you found this useful, consider sharing it with someone who would
benefit. Questions or corrections? Reach out.*

```

## Formatting conventions

- Use `**bold**` for key terms on first introduction
- Use `> blockquotes` for memorable quotes from sources or striking statistics
- Use `---` sparingly to separate major sections when transitioning
- Code snippets: inline `` `code` `` and fenced blocks for technical content
- Images: descriptive alt-text, caption below

## Title guidelines

- Specific beats clever: "Why Prompt Engineering Is Still Mostly Art, Not Science" outperforms "The Truth About AI"
- Numbers work when backed by data: "3 Findings That Change How We Think About X"
- Avoid ALL CAPS, excessive punctuation, or hype language

## What to avoid

- **Throat-clearing**: Do not open with "In today's fast-paced world..." or similar filler
- **Padding**: Every paragraph should advance the argument or provide evidence
- **Over-linking**: One or two relevant links per section; not every other word
- **Mixed metaphors**: Choose one analogy and develop it, don't layer multiple metaphors
- **Unearned conclusions**: Don't claim a finding is "game-changing" or "paradigm-shifting" unless the evidence truly supports that strength of claim
