# Dr. Brad Stanfield — Channel Deep-Dive

## Channel Overview

Dr. Brad Stanfield is a New Zealand-based general practitioner whose YouTube channel focuses on **evidence-based longevity medicine and supplement evaluation**. His approach is distinguished by his "show me the RCT" rigor: he systematically reviews the clinical trial evidence for supplements, drugs, and lifestyle interventions that claim to slow aging, then updates his personal protocol based on the best available data. Stanfield is known for his **willingness to change his mind publicly** — he has stopped taking supplements he previously recommended when new evidence emerged (resveratrol, NMN/NR, fisetin), and he documents these reversals transparently. His channel bridges the gap between longevity influencers (who often oversell preliminary evidence) and conservative physicians (who often dismiss the entire field). Stanfield's core framework is a tiered approach: **foundational interventions** with strong RCT support (exercise, sleep, nutrition, specific supplements like creatine and vitamin D) at the base; **promising interventions** with some human evidence (rapamycin, metformin, NAD+ precursors) in the middle; and **experimental interventions** with only mechanistic or animal evidence at the top, approached with extreme caution.

## What Makes Him Unique

- **"I stopped taking this" videos**: Stanfield's most distinctive content format — he publishes videos detailing which supplements he has removed from his protocol and why. This is the opposite of the typical influencer approach of perpetually adding more products. His "What I Stopped Taking" series has included resveratrol, NMN, NR, fisetin, quercetin, and others.
- **RCT-first philosophy**: While many longevity communicators lean heavily on mechanistic reasoning ("this pathway suggests it should work"), Stanfield demands human outcome data. He is particularly skeptical of animal lifespan studies and surrogate biomarkers that haven't been validated against hard endpoints.
- **Transparent cost-benefit analysis**: Stanfield openly discusses the financial cost of supplements, weighing it against the evidence strength. He's equally comfortable saying "this intervention costs $5/month and has decent evidence — worth it" and "this costs $100/month and the evidence is weak — skip it."
- **Primary care physician grounding**: Unlike PhD researchers who focus on a single pathway, Stanfield's medical practice keeps him grounded in what's actually practical and impactful for real patients. He consistently emphasizes that exercise, sleep, and nutrition swamp the effects of any supplement.
- **Modest presentation, no guru tendencies**: Stanfield presents as a doctor sharing his personal learning journey, not as a prophet of longevity. His thumbnails are understated, his claims are hedged, and he rarely overstates evidence.

## Recurring Themes & Frameworks

1. **Tiered intervention framework**: Foundational > Promising > Experimental. Allocate time and money accordingly.
2. **"What the RCTs show"**: His signature analytical move — summarize the randomized controlled trial evidence for a given intervention, with emphasis on human data over animal/mechanistic.
3. **Updating priors**: Stanfield explicitly models his approach on Bayesian reasoning — start with a prior (based on mechanism or preliminary evidence), update it as new studies emerge.
4. **Cost-effectiveness in longevity**: Not all interventions are worth the money. Stanfield factors cost into his recommendations.
5. **The foundational quartet**: Exercise (especially resistance training), sleep (7–8h), protein intake (1.2–1.6 g/kg), and calorie moderation/swapping — these come before any supplement.
6. **NAD+ precursor skepticism**: Stanfield was an early and persistent skeptic of NR and NMN, arguing the human evidence was weak while the marketing was strong. He later stopped taking NMN after the RCT data disappointed.
7. **Rapamycin and metformin cautiously considered**: Unlike many longevity enthusiasts, Stanfield approaches prescription geroprotective drugs with medical caution, emphasizing the need for more human data.
8. **Senolytics: interesting but premature**: He follows the fisetin, quercetin, and dasatinib+quercetin literature closely but has pulled back from personal use pending stronger evidence.

## Top 5 Videos That Best Synthesize His Teachings

### 1. My Complete Supplement and Longevity Protocol (Updated for 2024/2025)

- **URL**: Available on Dr. Brad Stanfield's YouTube channel (search "Dr Brad Stanfield supplement protocol")
- **Duration**: ~20–35 minutes
- **Published**: Updated annually (~2023, 2024, 2025)
- **Why This Matters**: This is Stanfield's **annual "state of the protocol" address** — a comprehensive walkthrough of every supplement, medication, and lifestyle intervention he personally uses, organized by evidence tier. It's his most synthesized and actionable video, updated as new evidence emerges. This is the single best entry point to his thinking and the video to watch before any other Stanfield content.
- **Key Takeaways** (from his most recent published protocol):
  - **Foundational (strong evidence, low cost/risk)**:
    - **Creatine monohydrate**: 5g/day — the best-evidenced supplement for muscle, strength, and potentially cognitive health. One of the few supplements Stanfield is enthusiastic about.
    - **Vitamin D3**: 2000–4000 IU/day — maintains serum 25(OH)D in the 40–60 ng/mL range. Especially important in winter or at high latitudes.
    - **Magnesium**: 200–400 mg/day (glycinate or malate) — supports sleep, muscle function, and vitamin D metabolism.
    - **Omega-3 (EPA/DHA)**: 1–2g/day from fish oil or algal oil — target omega-3 index >8%. Stanfield emphasizes that food sources (fatty fish 2–3×/week) are preferable to supplements.
    - **Protein powder** (whey or plant-based): To help reach the 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day protein target, especially post-exercise.
  - **Mid-tier (some evidence, monitoring required)**:
    - **Glycine + NAC (GlyNAC)**: 1–2g glycine + 600mg NAC — glutathione precursor combination. Some evidence for improved mitochondrial function and reduced oxidative stress in older adults, but more RCTs needed.
    - **Taurine**: 1–3g/day — associated with longevity in animal studies. Mechanistically plausible (mitochondrial function, bile acid conjugation, osmoregulation). Human evidence still developing.
    - **Collagen peptides**: 10–15g/day — for joint and skin health. Stanfield acknowledges the evidence is modest but the safety profile is excellent.
    - **Hyaluronic acid**: For skin hydration and joint health — modest but positive evidence.
  - **Prescription medications (doctor-supervised)**:
    - **Rapamycin**: Stanfield has experimented with intermittent low-dose rapamycin (weekly), citing the ITP (Interventions Testing Program) lifespan data in mice. However, he emphasizes this is experimental, requires medical supervision, and the side effect profile (mouth ulcers, possible immunosuppression) warrants caution.
    - **Metformin**: Stanfield reads the metformin evidence as mixed — the TAME trial (Targeting Aging with Metformin) is ongoing. He leans toward the view that metformin may blunt some exercise adaptations, making it a nuanced choice.
  - **What he STOPPED taking** (and why):
    - **NMN/NR**: The human RCTs didn't show the benefits the mouse studies and marketing promised. Stanfield concluded the bioavailability and efficacy data didn't justify the high cost.
    - **Resveratrol**: The human evidence collapsed after the initial hype (David Sinclair's early work was largely based on flawed in vitro assays). Stanfield dropped it years ago.
    - **Fisetin**: The senolytic evidence in humans is too preliminary. Stanfield is watching the space but no longer self-experiments.
    - **Quercetin**: Similar to fisetin — the senolytic human data isn't there yet.
- **Studies/Books/Channels Cited**:
  - ITP (Interventions Testing Program) — rapamycin lifespan extension in mice (NIA)
  - TAME trial (metformin — Nir Barzilai, Albert Einstein College of Medicine)
  - GlyNAC human trials (Sekhar et al., Baylor College of Medicine)
  - Creatine meta-analyses and position stands (ISSN)
  - NAD+ precursor RCTs (multiple — often disappointing results)
  - Omega-3 index and mortality data

### 2. NMN and NR: Why I Stopped Taking NAD+ Precursors

- **URL**: Available on Dr. Brad Stanfield's YouTube channel
- **Duration**: ~15–20 minutes
- **Published**: ~2022–2023
- **Why This Matters**: This is Stanfield's **most representative video** — it exemplifies his willingness to reverse course, his RCT-first methodology, and his cost-conscious approach. NAD+ precursors (NMN and NR) were one of the most hyped supplement categories in the longevity space, driven by David Sinclair's animal research and book. Stanfield was an early adopter, then stopped. This video explains why the human evidence didn't pan out and why he believes most people are wasting their money. It is the definitive public critique of NAD+ supplementation.
- **Key Takeaways**:
  - **The animal data was compelling**: In mice, NMN and NR improved NAD+ levels, mitochondrial function, exercise capacity, and in some studies extended lifespan. This generated enormous excitement.
  - **The human data was disappointing**: Multiple RCTs in humans showed that while NAD+ precursors DO increase blood NAD+ levels, they haven't consistently improved any meaningful outcome — no clear effects on muscle function, cognitive performance, insulin sensitivity, or exercise capacity. The effect sizes were small or null.
  - **NAD+ levels might not be rate-limiting in humans**: Unlike aged mice, humans may not have NAD+ deficiency driving dysfunction. Raising NAD+ when it isn't low doesn't provide a benefit.
  - **Cost is prohibitive**: NMN and NR are expensive ($50–100+/month for standard doses). Stanfield argues this money is better spent on interventions with stronger evidence: creatine, omega-3s, protein, exercise equipment, or higher-quality food.
  - **The exercise-NMN interaction**: Some evidence suggests exercise is a far more potent NAD+ booster than supplementation. Stanfield's take: just exercise.
  - **What would change his mind**: A large, well-designed RCT showing NMN/NR improves a hard endpoint (not just NAD+ levels) — muscle strength, VO2 max, or cognitive decline. Until then, he's out.
- **Studies/Books/Channels Cited**:
  - David Sinclair's NAD+ research (Harvard) — the foundational animal data
  - NMN/NR human RCTs (multiple — reviewed systematically)
  - Critique of the NAD+ precursor market from other researchers
  - Exercise and NAD+ physiology studies

### 3. The Supplements I Actually Take (and Why) — Minimalist Edition

- **URL**: Available on Dr. Brad Stanfield's YouTube channel
- **Duration**: ~15–20 minutes
- **Published**: ~2022–2023
- **Why This Matters**: In contrast to the maximalist "100+ supplement" approaches of some biohackers, Stanfield presents his **minimalist supplement strategy** — what you should take if you only want the highest-confidence, best-value supplements. This video is a pragmatic antidote to supplement overwhelm and is the one to watch if you want to start somewhere without spending hundreds per month.
- **Key Takeaways**:
  - **The "if I could only take 5" list**: Creatine, vitamin D3, magnesium, omega-3s, and protein powder. Everything else is negotiable.
  - **Why these 5?**: Each has multiple RCTs showing benefit on relevant endpoints, excellent safety profiles, and low cost. They address the most common nutritional gaps in modern diets.
  - **Creatine is the star**: Not just for muscle — emerging evidence for cognitive benefits, especially in older adults, vegetarians/vegans, and during sleep deprivation. It's cheap, safe, and effective.
  - **Vitamin D is population-dependent**: If you live near the equator and get daily sun, you may not need it. If you're in the UK/New Zealand/Northern US/Canada, you almost certainly do for at least half the year.
  - **Omega-3 from food first**: Stanfield strongly prefers fatty fish 2–3×/week over supplements. If you don't eat fish, algal oil is the best alternative (addresses sustainability and oxidation concerns with fish oil).
  - **Magnesium glycinate before bed**: For sleep quality and the ~50% of the population that doesn't meet magnesium RDA from diet.
  - **Protein powder as food, not supplement**: Stanfield categorizes protein powder as a convenient food source rather than a "supplement." The goal is hitting the protein target (1.2–1.6 g/kg/day), not taking a magic powder.
- **Studies/Books/Channels Cited**:
  - Creatine RCTs and meta-analyses
  - Vitamin D / omega-3 large trials (VITAL, D-Health, etc.)
  - Magnesium and sleep RCTs
  - Protein intake and aging studies

### 4. Rapamycin and Metformin: What the Evidence Says for Longevity

- **URL**: Available on Dr. Brad Stanfield's YouTube channel
- **Duration**: ~15–25 minutes
- **Published**: ~2023
- **Why This Matters**: Rapamycin and metformin are the two most discussed prescription drugs in the longevity community. Stanfield provides a sober, clinician's-eye review of both, cutting through the hype to assess what we actually know from human data. He's more bullish on rapamycin (cautiously) and more skeptical of metformin (due to the exercise-blunting concern), making this a nuanced take in a polarized debate.
- **Key Takeaways**:
  - **Rapamycin (mTOR inhibitor)**:
    - The ITP (Interventions Testing Program) showed rapamycin consistently extends lifespan in mice — including when started in middle age. This is one of the most robust signals in aging biology.
    - Human evidence is very limited. No longevity trials exist. Side effects include mouth ulcers, potential immunosuppression at higher doses, and metabolic effects (impaired glucose tolerance at high doses).
    - Stanfield's approach: intermittent low-dose (weekly, 5–6mg), under medical supervision. He monitors for side effects and emphasizes this is NOT for everyone.
    - The risk/reward calculus changes with age. A 70-year-old may accept more risk than a 30-year-old for an intervention that might extend healthspan.
  - **Metformin**:
    - In diabetic patients, metformin reduces all-cause mortality compared to other glucose-lowering drugs — this is the basis for longevity interest.
    - In non-diabetics, the evidence is weaker and more conflicting. The TAME trial (Nir Barzilai) is specifically designed to test metformin's effect on aging in non-diabetics — results pending.
    - **The exercise concern**: Some evidence suggests metformin blunts mitochondrial adaptations to exercise, reducing the benefits of training. For someone who exercises regularly (which Stanfield strongly advocates), metformin might be counterproductive.
    - Stanfield's tentative conclusion: more evidence needed. Currently, he leans away from metformin for non-diabetic exercisers.
  - **Neither is a substitute for lifestyle**: Stanfield closes by emphasizing that even if rapamycin or metformin prove to slow aging, their effects are dwarfed by exercise, sleep, and nutrition.
- **Studies/Books/Channels Cited**:
  - ITP (Interventions Testing Program) — NIA-funded rapamycin lifespan studies
  - TAME trial design and rationale (Nir Barzilai)
  - Metformin and exercise adaptation studies
  - UKPDS (UK Prospective Diabetes Study) — metformin mortality benefit in diabetics
  - Peter Attia's discussions of rapamycin (frequent cross-reference)
  - Matt Kaeberlein's rapamycin research (Dog Aging Project)

### 5. Senolytics: Fisetin, Quercetin, and the Quest to Clear Senescent Cells

- **URL**: Available on Dr. Brad Stanfield's YouTube channel
- **Duration**: ~15–20 minutes
- **Published**: ~2022–2023
- **Why This Matters**: Senolytics — drugs/supplements that selectively clear senescent ("zombie") cells — is one of the most exciting frontiers in aging biology. The Mayo Clinic's James Kirkland has shown that dasatinib+quercetin can improve physical function in aged mice and has conducted small human trials. Stanfield reviews the evidence for the most popular senolytic supplements (fisetin, quercetin) and explains why he stopped taking them. This video represents his **"promising but premature"** category.
- **Key Takeaways**:
  - **The senescent cell hypothesis**: As we age, cells enter senescence — they stop dividing but don't die, instead secreting inflammatory signals (SASP — senescence-associated secretory phenotype) that damage surrounding tissue. Clearing these cells improves tissue function in animal models.
  - **Dasatinib + Quercetin (D+Q)** is the best-studied senolytic combination, originally identified by the Mayo Clinic team (Kirkland, Tchkonia, et al.). Small human trials show improvements in physical function and reduced senescent cell burden in specific conditions (idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, diabetic kidney disease).
  - **Fisetin** (a flavonoid found in strawberries) showed senolytic activity in the Mayo screen and extended lifespan in mice. However, the human RCT evidence is very thin — small trials, mixed results. The cost of effective doses is high ($50–100+/month). Stanfield stopped taking it.
  - **Quercetin** (found in apples, onions, capers) — part of the D+Q combo. As a standalone supplement, human evidence for senolytic activity is minimal. Stanfield stopped taking it.
  - **Why he stopped**: Three reasons: (1) Human RCTs haven't convincingly shown benefits for healthy aging populations, (2) the optimal dosing protocol (how often? how much?) is unknown, (3) cost is high relative to evidence strength.
  - **What would change his mind**: Positive results from ongoing senolytic trials, particularly the large, NIH-funded studies. Stanfield is optimistic about the science but conservative about personal implementation.
- **Studies/Books/Channels Cited**:
  - James Kirkland (Mayo Clinic) — the foundational senolytic research
  - D+Q human trials (IPF, diabetic kidney disease)
  - Fisetin animal lifespan studies and human RCTs
  - The NIA's senolytic research program (Translational Geroscience Branch)
  - Unity Biotechnology senolytic drug trials (UBX-1325, etc.)
