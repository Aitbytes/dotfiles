# Codie Sanchez — Channel Deep Dive

## Channel Overview

Codie Sanchez is the founder of Contrarian Thinking, an investment and advisory firm, and the creator of the "boring business" movement. A former Goldman Sachs analyst and institutional investor, she built a portfolio of small businesses (laundromats, car washes, vending routes, trade franchises) using a counter-intuitive strategy: **buy existing cash-flowing businesses using the seller's own money (seller financing) instead of starting from scratch.** Her core thesis is that 90% of startups fail while 80% of acquisitions survive, that the average small business owner in the US is 67 years old and looking to exit, and that the greatest wealth-building opportunity of this generation is buying these "boring" businesses that nobody else wants. She's known for her "unfair" deal structures — buying businesses with little to no money down, using future profits to pay the seller.

## What Makes Her Unique

Sanchez occupies a distinctive and contrarian lane: (1) **Acquisition over creation** — while the entire internet tells you to "start a startup," she argues buying is statistically safer, faster, and more lucrative; the richest people in the world (Bezos, Musk, Branson) all grew through acquisitions. (2) **"Boring" as a feature, not a bug** — she champions businesses that are essential, recession-resistant, and too unglamorous for Silicon Valley to care about (waste management, plumbing, car washes, laundromats). (3) **The "OPX" framework** (Other People's X) — she teaches how to use other people's capital, resources, network, and credibility before deploying your own; a direct carryover from Goldman Sachs. (4) **Seller financing as the great equalizer** — she demystifies the mechanism by which a 67-year-old business owner will literally pay you to take over their business, because it gives them more money, less taxes, and faster closing. (5) **Finance-trained, not guru-trained** — her advice comes from institutional finance (Goldman, Vanguard, private equity), giving it a rigor and vocabulary that distinguishes her from typical business influencers.

## Recurring Themes & Frameworks

- **The Seven D's of Seller Motivation**: Why business owners sell — Departure (retirement, relocation), Divorce, Disease, Disagreement (partner conflicts), Distress (business declining), Death, and Dullness (boredom). Every seller falls into one of these buckets.
- **Seller Financing**: Instead of a bank loan, the seller acts as the bank. Buyer puts some money down, seller finances the rest. Benefits to seller: more total purchase price, tax advantages (installment sale vs. lump sum), faster closing. Benefits to buyer: less upfront capital, no bank qualification, faster deal.
- **The Deal Box**: Clearly defined acquisition criteria — industry, revenue range, profit margin, geography, deal size. Like a Zillow search for your perfect business.
- **On-Market vs. Off-Market**: On-market deals are listed on business broker sites (BizBuySell, etc.). Off-market deals require direct outreach — door knocking, cold calling, letter campaigns to business owners who aren't actively selling but match the Seven D's profile.
- **The Four Quadrants (Goldman Sachs Method)**: (1) Define your acquisition target (deal box), (2 & 3) Search on-market AND off-market, (4) Target and narrow down the right deals.
- **OPX — Other People's X**: Before using your own capital/resources/network, ask: whose capital can I use instead of mine? Whose resources? Whose network? If the answer to all three is "my own," you're not thinking about it right.
- **"Your first $500K is for losing"**: A Goldman mentor taught her that your first half-million of investing experience will be lost to mistakes. So lose somebody else's money first. This is why working for a big company before entrepreneurship is underrated.
- **The 20% Cut Rule**: Every year, cut the bottom 20% — of clients, habits, and relationships. High performers prune constantly; most people just accumulate.
- **Reputation as a compounding asset**: The story people tell about you when you leave the room either compounds or decays. Manage it actively; don't let others write your narrative.
- **"Boring" business advantages**: Recession-resistant, low tech disruption risk ("Amazon can't fix a basement from India"), reliable cash flow, aging owner demographic creating supply, minimal competition from "sexy" entrepreneurs.

### Video 1: How to Find Businesses to Buy NO ONE ELSE Knows About (80 MINUTE Playbook)

- **URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3mtS8rOAqoM
- **Duration**: 80 minutes (4,776 seconds)
- **Views**: ~404K
- **Published**: ~2024

**Why This Video Matters**: This is Sanchez's definitive masterclass — the single most comprehensive video on her channel. It covers the complete end-to-end process of finding, evaluating, and structuring the purchase of a small business using seller financing. At 80 minutes, it's her longest video and the closest thing to a full course on acquisition entrepreneurship. She frames it as "stealing my 15 years of finance homework."

**Key Takeaways**:
- **Acquisitions beat startups**: 70% of US millionaires own a business; 88% of those worth $30M+ did an acquisition. 90% of startups fail vs. 80% of acquisitions survive year one. SBA-financed acquisitions have a ~13% failure rate. Every Forbes 100 billionaire has done acquisitions (Amazon: 110, Google: 200+)
- **The Seven D's framework**: The 7 reasons sellers sell — Departure (avg. small business owner is 67), Divorce, Disease, Disagreement (partner fights), Distress, Death (probate), Dullness (boredom). These create motivated sellers
- **Seller financing mechanics in detail**: Walk through a real deal — seller wants $1M, bank values at $750K. Option A: bank loan at 8% = buyer pays $750K + ~$80K/yr interest. Option B: seller finances = buyer offers $1.15M total ($1M purchase + $150K interest), spread over years at lower rate. Seller gets $1.46M vs. $750K. "I'd rather have $750K × 2 than $750K."
- **Tax advantage of seller financing**: Lump sum sale = big immediate tax bill. Installment sale over 10 years = lower tax bracket since seller isn't a business owner anymore. Saves thousands to hundreds of thousands.
- **"Time kills all deals"**: Bank/SBA loans take 3-9 months. Seller financing closes in 2-3 weeks. Faster = less chance of deal falling apart.
- **The Goldman Sachs Four Quadrants**: (1) Define your deal box (industry, revenue, profit, geography, size), (2) On-market search (broker sites like BizBuySell), (3) Off-market search (direct outreach), (4) Target and narrow
- **Off-market is where the best deals are**: On-market deals are shopped to everyone and priced competitively. Off-market deals — businesses whose owners match the Seven D's but haven't listed — have less competition and more flexible terms
- **How to present yourself as a trusted buyer**: Sellers care about more than price — they care about legacy, employees, continuation of the business. Your presentation as a buyer matters as much as your offer
- **The Sam Zell quote**: "Some see buying and creating value from others' misfortunes as exploitation, but I see it as giving neglected or devalued assets in any industry a new life"
- **Deal tracking system**: How to build a pipeline of potential acquisitions, track outreach, and follow up systematically

- Goldman Sachs (institutional acquisition methodology)
- SBA (Small Business Administration) loan statistics

### Video 2: Everything They Teach You At Goldman Sachs

- **URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PbBdBpBsP_s
- **Duration**: 17 minutes (1,039 seconds)
- **Views**: ~Not resolved
- **Published**: May 21, 2026

**Why This Video Matters**: This is Sanchez's most distilled transmission of institutional finance wisdom to individual entrepreneurs. Having worked at Goldman Sachs — where "the world's most powerful governments, corporations, and billionaires go to figure out what to do with their money" — she extracts the five lessons that changed how she builds wealth. It's intensely practical and unusually philosophical for business content.

**Key Takeaways**:
- **Lesson 1: Your first $500K is for losing — lose somebody else's money**: A Goldman mentor told her "your first half a million dollars of investing experience is for losing." Big institutions have risk budgets — they budget for your learning-curve mistakes. Your personal savings don't have that buffer. "The most dangerous advice circling the internet right now is just go be an entrepreneur tomorrow... statistically it's actually terrible advice for most people." Learn on someone else's dime first.
- **The OPX Check** (Other People's X): Before any major move, ask 3 questions — Whose capital can I use instead of mine? Whose resources? Whose network? If the answer to all three is "my own," you're not thinking about it right
- **Lesson 2: Cut your bottom 20% — every year**: Goldman fires the bottom 20% annually. Every high performer does this, just quieter. Annual audit of: clients (cut the ones costing energy), habits (cut the ones deciding your years), circle (cut people capping your ceiling). "Every high performer has to cut to make room to grow."
- **The "Jack" story**: A colleague spent 6 months engineering the appearance of better numbers instead of fixing actual performance. Got fired with a bad recommendation ("basically like a career bomb"). A decade later, "not living his best life." He didn't lack talent — he couldn't face the decision and do the hard work instead
- **Lesson 3: Be honest even when you're about to publicly fail**: The most expensive mistake at Goldman wasn't a bad trade — it was lying about it. "We all manage how it looks instead of fixing what it is." People respect "I'm failing at this. Here's why. Here's what I'm doing about it" more than someone caught in a lie
- **"Tell the truth even when it's going to cost you"** — her father's advice. Every person she watched choose to lie "never came back from it." Those who owned failures went on to do huge things
- **Lesson 4: Your reputation is a compounding asset**: "'I don't care what people think' is one of the most expensive lies you can tell yourself." Goldman got crushed in public opinion during the financial crisis not because they were the worst actor, but because they let others write their narrative. Your reputation either compounds or decays — manage it actively
- **Lesson 5: Stop trying to get into easy doors**: Goldman made you go through 7-12 brutal interviews on purpose. Hard doors filter out everyone who isn't serious, which means the people inside are the ones you want to be around. "If an opportunity is easy to get, everybody's already in there"

**References & Citations**:
- Goldman Sachs (institutional practices, recruitment process, culture)
- 2008 Financial Crisis (Countrywide vs. Goldman narrative)
- Personal: 7-12 interviews to get into Goldman

### Video 4: How To Think Like The 1%

- **URL**: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_VtdNIkdWBs
- **Duration**: 30 minutes (1,785 seconds)
- **Views**: ~Not resolved
- **Published**: September 26, 2024

**Why This Video Matters**: This is Sanchez's mindset and wealth psychology video — the mental models that distinguish how the wealthy approach money, risk, and opportunity. It bridges her institutional finance background with practical application, addressing not just *what* to do but *how to think* about wealth-building differently from 99% of people.

**Key Takeaways**:
- The wealthy think in terms of asset acquisition, not income generation — the goal is to own things that produce cash flow, not to earn a higher salary
- Risk is asymmetrically misunderstood: most people avoid all risk; the wealthy seek asymmetric risk (limited downside, unlimited upside)
- The "time vs. money" inversion: poor people trade time for money; wealthy people trade money for time, then use that time to find more assets
- Cash flow > net worth: a $10M net worth in illiquid assets is less powerful than $2M in cash-flowing businesses that replace your income
- "The wealthy are boring" — extreme wealth rarely comes from flashy businesses; it comes from systematic acquisition of mundane cash-flowing assets
- The importance of being a "buyer" not a "seller" in every transaction — always be on the acquiring side of the table
- How to evaluate opportunity cost like an investor: every dollar and hour has an alternative use; the wealthy are ruthless about this calculation
