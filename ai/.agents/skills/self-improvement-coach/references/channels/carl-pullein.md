# Carl Pullein — Channel Deep-Dive

## Channel Overview

**Channel:** [Carl Pullein](https://www.youtube.com/@Carl_Pullein)  
**Host:** Carl Pullein — productivity coach, author, 30+ year veteran of productivity systems  
**Core Philosophy:** The fundamental flaw in modern productivity is that we organize by *what* we need to do (task-based systems) rather than *when* we can do it (time-based systems). Carl's Time Sector System replaces complex tagging, due dates, and priority matrices with a single question: "When am I going to do this?" His approach is pragmatic, battle-tested over three decades, and refreshingly minimalist — he argues most productivity problems come from overcomplicating systems, not from personal failure.

### What Makes Him Unique
- **30+ years of productivity experimentation:** Carl has lived through every major productivity movement — paper planners in the '80s, FranklinCovey, Palm Pilots, GTD, digital task managers, and now AI. His perspective is deeply historical and skeptical of fads.
- **The GTD apostate:** Carl followed David Allen's *Getting Things Done* for many years before concluding it "didn't work for him" — not because GTD is bad, but because task-based organization inevitably creates backlog and overwhelm. His Time Sector System is a direct, explicit alternative.
- **Time-first, not task-first:** His core insight is counterintuitive: "It doesn't matter how much you have to do if you don't have the time to do it." Every other system organizes by project, priority, or context. Carl organizes by time horizon: this week, next week, this month, next month, long-term.
- **British pragmatism:** Carl's delivery is dry, direct, and skeptical of over-optimization. He frequently says things like "don't overthink this" and "keep it simple." His advice is about reducing cognitive load, not adding frameworks.
- **Todoist mastery:** While tool-agnostic in principle, Carl is one of the foremost Todoist experts on YouTube, with setup guides that show exactly how to implement the Time Sector System in a real app.
- **COD (Collect, Organise, Do):** His three-part recovery framework for when systems break — simpler than GTD, designed as a reset protocol, not a permanent methodology.

### Recurring Frameworks & Themes
1. **Time Sector System (TSS):** Organize tasks by *when* you'll do them, not *what* they are. Folders: This Week, Next Week, This Month, Next Month, Long-term/On Hold. The only decision during processing: "When am I going to do this?"
2. **COD Method:** **C**ollect everything into a trusted place, **O**rganise it by when you'll do it (5-10 minutes at end of day), **D**o the work — one thing at a time. This is the recovery/reset protocol.
3. **Weekly Planning (not Weekly Review):** Carl distinguishes sharply between reviewing (looking backward, GTD-style, 3-4 hours) and planning (setting 3-4 objectives for the week ahead, 15-30 minutes). Planning is forward-looking; reviewing is optional.
4. **Daily Planning:** 5-10 minutes, evening or morning. Look at appointments, calculate available time for tasks, select what fits. "You can only do one thing at a time."
5. **Calendar as Sacred Territory:** "What goes on my calendar gets done." Deadlines live on the calendar (all-day events), not in the task manager. The task manager is for "random stuff I may or may not do."
6. **One Life, One System:** Don't separate work and personal tasks. You have one life. The artificial division creates more problems than it solves.
7. **The Long-Term folder is for eliminating, not accumulating:** When you review older tasks, the goal is to delete, not revive. Most ideas that seemed good three months ago turn out to be irrelevant.
8. **Projects in Notes, Tasks in Task Manager:** Projects are managed in a notes app (where you can store emails, documents, screenshots, context). The task manager only has a single task: "Work on Project X." This prevents task manager bloat.
9. **Manual weekly task movement:** Don't automate moving tasks from "Next Week" to "This Week." The manual act of weekly planning is where intentionality happens. Automation creates backlog and overwhelm.
10. **Simplicity over sophistication:** Carl's most repeated advice: "Don't overthink this." The system should reduce anxiety, not create more decisions.

### 1. The Only Productivity System You'll Ever Need

- **Title:** The Only Productivity System You'll Ever Need
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gfcKbCwXIWw
- **Duration:** 16:50
- **Published:** April 9, 2025
- **Caption Confidence:** High (youtube-transcript-api)

**Why This Video Matters:** This is the most comprehensive FAQ/Q&A about the Time Sector System, released for its 5th anniversary. It answers the most common questions from thousands of course students and distills Carl's philosophy into practical, specific answers. The title is bold but earned — this really is his definitive statement on productivity.

**Key Takeaways:**
1. **The Time Sector System defined:** "A task management system that's based around *when* you will do a task rather than *what* you have to do." Only three questions in processing: What is it? What do I need to do? When am I going to do it?
2. **Time sectors:** This Week → Next Week → This Month → Next Month → Long-term/On Hold. Tasks are filed into exactly one sector based on when you'll realistically do them. No tags, no priorities, no energy levels, no contexts. Just time.
3. **Projects live in your notes app, not your task manager:** "A more logical, natural place to manage projects." You can throw emails, documents, screenshots, message links — anything — into a notes app. The task manager only needs a single recurring task: "Work on Project X."
4. **Simple project tasks can bypass the notes app:** A quick follow-up email or call related to a project can go directly into the task manager with the project name in brackets. No need to open the project note for every micro-task.
5. **Manual weekly planning is critical — don't automate:** "If you try and automate this, that's how you're going to start building up backlogs and overwhelm because you've not intentionally decided." Once a week, sit down, open your calendar, see your available time, and MANUALLY move tasks from Next Week/This Month into This Week. The manual touch IS the intentionality.
6. **Watch your ambition when planning the week:** Most people overestimate what they can do. Always have your calendar open during weekly planning. If you were at the dentist all day (Carl's example: 3-hour drive + 1-hour appointment + 3-hour drive back = 7 hours gone), don't pretend you'll get office work done.
7. **Don't separate work and personal tasks:** "You have one life." A task needs doing regardless of category. If you need to call your bank, you can only do it during office hours. If you have a meeting across time zones, you might work at 7 PM. "There's a bit of give and take." Exception: companies with strict security policies that block third-party task managers.
8. **Email tasks — two categories:**
   - **Simple replies/reading:** Go into an "Action This Day" folder in email. Don't clutter the task manager.
   - **Multi-hour work:** Move the email into the task manager. Most task managers integrate with email apps. Always reply to the sender giving a realistic timeline (err on the long side) — this puts you in control and lets them object if they need it sooner.
9. **The Long-term/On Hold folder is for eliminating, not accumulating:** Carl reviews his quarterly for 20-30 minutes. The goal is to DELETE, not revive. "What was I thinking about?" is a common reaction. "The Long-term and On Hold folder is about eliminating, not accumulating."
10. **Deadlines go on the calendar, not the task manager:** "The calendar is the sacred territory. What goes on my calendar gets done." Carl uses all-day events for deadlines. This has been his method since the 1990s. Due dates in task managers create anxiety: "I could feel a level of anxiety rising just by seeing that I'm running out of time."

### 2. All You Need to Get Organised AND Stay Organised

- **Title:** All You Need to Get Organised AND Stay Organised
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G1FnZKdSboc
- **Duration:** 11:07
- **Published:** March 4, 2026
- **Caption Confidence:** High (youtube-transcript-api)

**Why This Video Matters:** This is Carl's "reset" video — what to do when your system breaks (and it will). He introduces the COD method and clearly distinguishes between daily planning, weekly planning, and weekly review — three concepts that most productivity gurus conflate. The video is particularly valuable for its compassionate framing: when things go wrong, "it doesn't mean that our systems are failing."

**Key Takeaways:**
1. **COD — Collect, Organise, Do:** When overwhelmed, get back to these three basics:
   - **Collect:** Everything that might matter into ONE trusted place (notebook, Todoist, Things, Apple Reminders, any tool).
   - **Organise:** 5-10 minutes at the end of each work day. Look at what you collected and decide WHEN you will do each item. "The most important aspect of when you're organizing your stuff is deciding when you are going to do it."
   - **Do:** Start with one thing. "You can only do one thing at a time."
2. **When things go wrong, the system isn't broken — life happened:** "Sometimes, no matter how carefully organized and planned we are, things will go wrong. It's an inevitability of life. That doesn't mean that our systems are failing." The antidote is returning to basics, not buying new tools.
3. **Weekly Planning ≠ Weekly Review (critical distinction):**
   - **Weekly Review (GTD-style):** Looking BACKWARD at what you've done, trying to remember all commitments. Can take 3-4 hours. Optional.
   - **Weekly Planning:** Looking FORWARD. Setting 3-4 objectives for the week. "I'm going to exercise a minimum of four times this week" or "I'm going to push this particular project forward because the deadline is coming up." 15-30 minutes. Essential.
4. **Objectives, not tasks, at the weekly level:** "I see a lot of people trying to plan out tasks when they do their weekly planning. Well, that's going to be a game of guessing because you don't know what's going to happen on Monday morning or Tuesday afternoon." Set objectives at the weekly level; tasks are for daily planning.
5. **Daily planning — 5-10 minutes, evening or morning:** "All you're doing is: where are my appointments for today, and what time do I have available to do my tasks?" Carl plans the night before. The daily plan is about fitting tasks into the space left by appointments.
6. **You're almost certainly behind — that's normal:** "Let's be honest, most people are behind with their work. It's not any fault of us, it's just the volume of work that's coming at us." The goal isn't to get caught up — it's to keep your head calm and maximize the time you do have.
7. **Buffer time is essential:** Allow time between tasks for overruns and emergencies. This is not wasted time — it's insurance against the day falling apart.
8. **The organizing step is about WHEN, not WHAT:** "We all have a lot of stuff to do. It's more about when you are going to do it." This is the thread that connects all of Carl's teaching.
9. **Don't overthink the tool:** "A lot of people overthink this process." Any tool works — Todoist, Things, TickTick, Apple Reminders, Microsoft To Do, Trello. The system matters, not the app.
10. **Recovery, not reinvention:** When systems bloat, "it's just been a large wave of work coming in that has kind of bloated your system." Don't abandon your system — just return to COD basics until you're back to baseline.

### 3. A Better Way To Manage Your Workload

- **Title:** A Better Way To Manage Your Workload
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_oOcSUlx3RU
- **Duration:** 11:08
- **Published:** May 7, 2020
- **Caption Confidence:** High (youtube-transcript-api)

**Why This Video Matters:** This is one of Carl's earliest and most foundational videos on the time-based approach. It makes the simplest possible case for why task-based systems fail: no task gets done without time, so organizing by anything other than time is fundamentally misaligned. The production is simpler than his later work, but the argument is crystal clear.

**Key Takeaways:**
1. **The single variable that matters:** "When it comes to managing the work you need to do, the only thing that matters is available time. No task gets complete unless you have the time to do it." This sounds obvious but contradicts every major productivity system (GTD, Eisenhower Matrix, Kanban) which organizes by priority, project, or status.
2. **Task-based systems create the illusion of control:** A long list sorted by project or priority looks organized but answers the wrong question. You can know everything you have to do and still accomplish nothing because you never asked "when?"
3. **Time is the constraint, not tasks:** Most productivity systems assume the bottleneck is knowing what to do. Carl argues the bottleneck is having time to do it. The system should surface what fits into available time, not everything that exists.
4. **The Time Sector Method introduction:** Tasks are filed into time-based buckets. You only look at what's in your "This Week" bucket. Everything else is out of sight until planning sessions. This prevents the "overwhelm scroll" through hundreds of tasks.
5. **Weekly planning as the "when" decision:** Once a week, you decide which tasks from future buckets move into "This Week" based on actual available time (not ambition). This is the critical feedback loop that keeps the system calibrated to reality.
6. **The calendar as the time-truth source:** Your calendar shows committed time. Subtract meetings and appointments. The remaining time is what you have for tasks. Any system that doesn't consult the calendar first is operating on fantasy.
7. **Simple processing question:** When a new task arrives: "When will I do this — this week, next week, this month, or later?" Nothing else. Not "how important?" or "what project?" Just when.
8. **The early formulation of TSS:** This video captures the Time Sector System before it was fully branded and packaged. It's the raw argument, closer to the original insight that launched the course.
9. **Overwhelm comes from seeing everything at once:** The psychological benefit of time-sectoring is that you only see what you've committed to for this week. The hundreds of other tasks exist but they're filed away, not in your face.
10. **Realism beats optimism:** The system forces you to confront how much you can actually do in a week. Over time, this calibrates your expectations and reduces the gap between planned and actual output.

### 4. The Ultimate Todoist Setup: Filters, Labels, and Time Sectors

- **Title:** The Ultimate Todoist Setup: Filters, Labels, and Time Sectors
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1aXKbT8nvrE
- **Duration:** 21:53
- **Published:** December 17, 2025
- **Caption Confidence:** High (youtube-transcript-api)

**Why This Video Matters:** This is Carl's most detailed walkthrough of how to implement the Time Sector System in Todoist — the tool he's used for over 10 years. It shows the theory in practice: exact folder structures, label schemes, and filter configurations. For anyone who wants to adopt Carl's system, this is the implementation manual.

**Key Takeaways:**
1. **Todoist as the reference implementation:** Carl has used Todoist for over a decade. His setup represents the "canonical" implementation of the Time Sector System in a digital task manager.
2. **The project structure mirrors time sectors:** Projects (folders in Todoist) are: This Week, Next Week, This Month, Next Month, Long-term, On Hold — exactly the time horizons from the system. No work/personal split. No area-of-life folders.
3. **The Apple Reminders experiment and return to Todoist:** Carl tried switching to Apple Reminders but returned to Todoist because of reliability issues. The tool matters less than the system, but reliability matters enormously — if you can't trust your system to hold your tasks, the whole methodology breaks.
4. **Filters serve the daily workflow:** Custom Todoist filters show only what you need at the right moment: "Today's Tasks" (from This Week, filtered for today), "This Week's Tasks" (everything in the This Week project), "Inbox" (unprocessed items). Filters reduce cognitive load by hiding everything else.
5. **Labels for optional context, not required:** While Carl generally advises against over-complicating with labels, he uses a few minimal labels (like "waiting" for delegated items) that don't add friction. The key is: labels are optional flavoring, not the organizing principle.
6. **The weekly planning ritual in Todoist:** During weekly planning, Carl manually drags tasks from Next Week/This Month into This Week. This is a deliberate, intentional act — not an automated rule. Todoist's drag-and-drop makes this fast.
7. **Inbox processing is daily:** New tasks land in the Todoist inbox. At the end of each day, Carl processes the inbox: decide when each item will be done, file into the appropriate time sector. 5-10 minutes.
8. **The "Today" view is sacred:** Todoist's Today view only shows tasks that are actually scheduled for today. Carl doesn't overpopulate it — he's realistic about what fits given his calendar commitments.
9. **Recurrence for habits and maintenance:** Recurring tasks (review long-term folder, weekly planning, daily planning) are set up as repeating tasks in Todoist. The system maintains itself through built-in prompts.
10. **Simplicity as the design principle:** Carl's Todoist setup is intentionally sparse. No complex tag hierarchies, no nested project structures, no automation. "Don't overthink this" applies to tool configuration too.

### 5. How I Manage My Workload (in under 15 minutes)

- **Title:** How I Manage My Workload (in under 15 minutes)
- **URL:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VwDwNr9U3_o
- **Duration:** 14:53
- **Published:** April 1, 2026
- **Caption Confidence:** High (youtube-transcript-api)

**Why This Video Matters:** This is Carl's most recent deep dive into the COD Framework — what he calls "the foundations of any solid productivity system." It addresses the most common failure mode: people collect information (screenshots, articles, "must-watch" videos) but never organize or do anything with it. The video is a practical guide to breaking that cycle.

**Key Takeaways:**
1. **The collection trap:** "The struggle isn't usually collecting the information; it's knowing how to process it." Most people have brilliant collection habits (bookmarks, screenshots, saved posts) and terrible processing habits. COD solves this.
2. **Collect into ONE trusted place:** Whether digital or paper, everything goes to one inbox. The key is trust — you must believe that if something is in the inbox, it will be processed. Without trust, you'll keep things in your head, defeating the purpose.
3. **Organise = decide WHEN:** The organising step is not about categorizing, tagging, or prioritizing. It's one decision: when will I do this? This week, next week, this month, or later. That's it. Anything more is over-engineering.
4. **The end-of-day organising ritual:** 5-10 minutes at the end of the work day. Process the inbox. File into time sectors. This is the keystone habit that prevents inbox bloat.
5. **Do — one thing at a time:** The "Do" step is straightforward but undermined by multitasking. "You can only do one thing at a time. Trying to do multiple things and saying yes to everything, you are kind of causing yourself a lot of trouble."
6. **COD as a recovery protocol:** When your system has bloated — too many tasks, too many projects, too much inbox — COD strips everything back to fundamentals. It's not a new system; it's the emergency reset.
7. **The COD → Daily Planning → Weekly Planning progression:** COD gets you back to baseline. Then daily planning keeps you on track day-to-day. Then weekly planning sets objectives. This is the complete cycle.
8. **Stop saving things "just in case":** The biggest COD failure mode is never deleting. Carl's rule: if you haven't looked at it in a week and it's not scheduled, seriously consider whether it matters. Most saved things don't.
9. **Information vs. action:** Not everything you collect is a task. Articles to read go to a read-later app (not the task manager). Videos to watch go to Watch Later. COD is for actionable items only — things you actually need to DO.
10. **The 15-minute promise:** The title isn't clickbait. Once you've set up the system (which takes an initial investment), daily maintenance genuinely takes under 15 minutes: 5-10 minutes for organising, 5 minutes for daily planning. The system is designed for sustainability, not heroics.
